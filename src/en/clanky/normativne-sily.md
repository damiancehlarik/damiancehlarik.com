Most people do not live among design icons but in an environment shaped by norms, tables and type systems. The dimensions of a kitchen unit, the number of rooms according to household size or the recommended colour of a child's room are not authorial gestures. Someone designed, tested and wrote them down. Damián Cehlárik's dissertation A Flat Is Never Finished: History as a Design Method and Housing Norms in Czechoslovakia in the Second Half of the 20th Century addresses this layer, which shapes everyday life the most yet remains almost invisible in teaching and in design history.

In this work a norm is any rule that determines in advance what a space, an object or a behaviour should be, including the unspoken idea of who will live in the flat. It is designed just like a chair: it has an author, an intended user and predecessors. It differs from an object in three ways. It does not design a thing but other people's decisions. It is almost invisible, because it appears self-evident. And it usually outlives the institution that wrote it.

The case is the Institute of Housing and Clothing Culture (ÚBOK), which from 1959 until its dissolution in 1990 designed the frameworks of housing and clothing in Czechoslovakia. The research draws on its internal documents in the library of the Museum of Decorative Arts in Prague (studies, methodologies, forecasts and the bulletin Bytová kultura) and on interviews with witnesses. A study from 1985, for example, recommends colours according to the temperament, age and occupation of residents, orange for housewives and for adolescents. Yet the same documents also reveal doubt. The institute was capable of questioning its own assumptions and wrote those doubts down. The knowledge existed; it was lost along the way.

The title comes from a sentence by Adolf Loos. Loos wrote it in 1898 in the past tense, recalling the flat of his childhood that grew together with the family. Almost eighty years later ÚBOK quoted it in the present tense to defend the adaptable flat. A sentence aimed against design from above thus ended up in a document of an institute that designed from above.

Historicism here means neither the nineteenth-century style nor the belief in a lawful course of history that Karl Popper criticised. It is a method by which a designer reads the origins of the norms their practice relies on and consciously decides what to adopt, change, reactivate or abandon. It builds on Jan Michl's concept of design as redesign and on Maria Göransdotter's transitional design histories. The history of norms is not a path to an inevitable goal but a series of decisions that could have turned out differently. That is exactly why they can be redesigned.

The introduction and the first two chapters, History as Method and The Norm as Design, exist in a working draft. The second follows the dispute over the type in the Werkbund, the minimum dwelling of the interwar avant-garde and the open norm that lived on in the Czechoslovak tradition but never reached production. Prototype chapters follow, essays around a single concept. The Inhabitant asks what kind of person the norms assumed and how people actually used their flats, from the kitchen and colour to the child. The Designed Future reads ÚBOK's forecasts as provisional designs posing as descriptions of the future and connects them with speculative design. Both have a first draft. The chapters on ÚBOK as an institution and on who designs and how it is taught rest on collected sources.

The work ends with a test. Workshops with AFAD students will try out the method: from an ÚBOK document they read what kind of person the norm assumed and design its redesign for today. The research follows on from the graduation project Housing Society at Design Academy Eindhoven and continues in the project Landscape of the City, which extends the question of the designed environment beyond the flat.
