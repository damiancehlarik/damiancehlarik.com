The web usually presents itself as immaterial. We speak of the cloud, of streams and of content, as if text and images existed apart from things. In A Prehistory of the Cloud, Tung-Hui Hu shows that the cloud is a metaphor that conceals cables, data centres and electricity. This website does the opposite. Every page shows how much it weighs and how much carbon it costs to view. Weight is not a technical detail here but the first piece of information about the medium.

The medium is not a neutral carrier. McLuhan's phrase that the medium is the message is taken literally here: the way a text reaches its reader is part of what it says. Friedrich Kittler went further when he wrote that media determine our situation, and when he argued that there is no software, only voltages in hardware. Behind every page there is a physical process that can be measured.

In A Geology of Media, Jussi Parikka traces media back to the earth: to the metals that are mined, the energy they consume and the waste they leave behind, which Jennifer Gabrys calls digital rubbish. Designing a website is therefore also a decision about material, even if it cannot be seen. Every kilobyte passes through a network, is processed by a device and adds to the pressure to replace it.

The images are 1-bit duotones. Each dot is either colour or paper, and tones arise only from scattering dots according to the Floyd–Steinberg algorithm of 1976. In her essay In Defense of the Poor Image, Hito Steyerl defends the poor image that trades quality for accessibility and movement. Here the poverty of the image is deliberate and visible: the grain admits that the image has passed through a medium. The solar-powered website of Kris De Decker's Low←Tech Magazine, the main model for this site, works the same way. This page has no image at all.

The website is static. Pages are made once, at build time, and the server simply passes them on. There is no database, no visitor tracking and no script running in the browser. Even the expanding CV is done by HTML alone. In 1948 Claude Shannon separated information from meaning. This website attempts the opposite: that as many transmitted bits as possible carry meaning rather than overhead.

The typeface is not downloaded at all. The website uses Georgia, a serif that Matthew Carter designed for Microsoft in the 1990s specifically for low-resolution screens. It comes with the system on Mac and Windows computers as well as on iPhones and iPads, so the browser simply takes it from the disk. Android does not have Georgia and shows Noto Serif, an open typeface by Google, instead. The website therefore does not look exactly the same everywhere. It is a deliberate trade: small differences in letterforms for about 220 kilobytes less on every view.

In Updating to Remain the Same, Wendy Hui Kyong Chun describes new media as habit, constant updates that keep us in place. A small website is an attempt to step out of that habit. It has no feed and no notifications, and the Index is a map of topics, not a timeline.

In The Stack, Benjamin Bratton describes computation as layers that reshape the planet, from energy and earth to the interface and the user. A small page is a small intervention in all layers at once. It thus belongs to a line also pursued by Laura U. Marks's Small File Media Festival, which understands small files not only as saving but as an aesthetic.

Weight and carbon are calculated by the site build every time it is published. Emissions follow the Sustainable Web Design Model v4: about 0.30 kWh per gigabyte transferred and a global average of 494 g CO₂e per kWh. It is an estimate, not a measurement. Today the largest part of the weight is the images. That too belongs to the case study: the website shows its own compromises.

The website was designed and developed by the studio Damian Works. The code is public. Like the studio's other work, it does not emerge as the gesture of a single author but as a post-authorial process whose traces can be followed.
